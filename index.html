How this game meets the project

Project objective

AERO 2.12.b

Analyze the causes and effects of events and developments in past eras, including the growth of trade routes and the spread of ideas, religions, cultures, and other developments.

How the game teaches it

Cause and effect is the game’s core mechanic rather than a quiz topic. Monsoon winds cause changes in sailing conditions, which change travel time and store use, which change whether a crew can trade and return. A decision made in the first minute is still shaping the last leg of the voyage, and the ending report names those links explicitly.

  • Cause: seasonal wind reversal, reef strike, storm front, illness at a connected port.
  • Development: faster or slower passage, hull damage, closed market window, lost crew time.
  • Your decision: three historically grounded responses, never labelled correct or wrong.
  • Effect: immediate resources and route change.
  • Future consequence: the same flag changes a later hazard, price, or survival roll.

Networks of exchange used

    Historical reasoning used

    Causation

    Departure timing, navigation tools, the rock repair, storm response, and paid protection each cause a later physical outcome.

    Comparison

    The route chart compares Indian Ocean, Silk Road, and Trans-Saharan networks by environment, goods, and range before the player picks a line.

    Contextualization

    Every decision is framed by season, hull condition, stores, and port politics rather than by an abstract rule.

    Continuity and change

    The ending compares the ship that left Kilwa with the ship that returned while noting the unchanged need to trade.

    Multiple perspectives

    Technology and innovation

    Tools are mechanics, not collectibles. Choosing the compass, astrolabe, and lateen sail raises navigation accuracy and shortens the route; a paid pilot widens safe passages; ignoring the wind angle costs control.

    Historical legacy

    Because these routes stayed connected for centuries, the habits built along them outlived any single voyage. Merchant communities, multilingual ports, shared legal norms for safe harbour, and the movement of faiths, technologies, and crops shaped later economic and cultural ties across Afro-Eurasia. The game makes that legacy playable: the crew you inherit is the crew a real merchant house would have trained, and the knowledge you carry home is the product of the voyage.

    Written component

    Networks of Exchange in the game

    The project centres on the Indian Ocean maritime route, and it supports that route with two comparison networks so students can see that exchange systems responded to their environments. The Indian Ocean network is the playable one: monsoon seasonality, reef coasts, and storms set the rhythm of travel, and its recurring goods — pepper, cotton cloth, glazed ceramics, and ivory — move because ports link East Africa, Arabia, and South Asia. The Silk Roads and the Trans-Saharan routes appear on the route chart and in the comparison decision, where a student weighs overland and desert logic against ocean conditions. A second exchange type, cultural diffusion, is modelled through the multilingual port decision, where conduct changes trust and the safety of the return passage. Disease is handled once, at Calicut, and kept non-graphic: a clerk reports illness at another connected port, and the player chooses how much contact to risk.

    Historical reasoning processes

    Four processes are built into play rather than bolted on afterwards. Causation appears in the repair decision that follows a reef strike and in the storm and protection responses, where an immediate resource change causes a measurable later effect. Comparison drives the pre-voyage route-planning decision that sets the player against the three networks and asks which environmental logic transfers to open water. Contextualization frames the departure and return-timing choices inside season, hull condition, and market politics. Continuity and change closes the game with a side-by-side comparison of the ship that left Kilwa and the ship that returned.

    Multiple interpretations and perspectives

    The crew does not hold one voice. The merchant wants margin, the captain wants a sound hull, the navigator wants a fair wind and a clear line, and the Calicut trader wants a quick, honest exchange. Each is represented as a speaker whose priorities conflict, so the student must decide whose priority to serve. The voyage report then asks which framework produced the strongest outcome, which keeps interpretation open rather than awarding a single answer.

    Technology, innovation, and legacy

    Period technologies change the simulation directly: the magnetic compass and mariner’s astrolabe raise navigational accuracy, the lateen sail lets a ship work close to the wind, and a coastal pilot widens safe passages at the cost of distance. The long-term legacy of these networks is stated at the end: sustained connection produced merchant diasporas, multilingual ports, shared rules about safe harbour, and the spread of faiths, technologies, and crops that linked Afro-Eurasia long after the medieval period.       How to play

      ☷Setup & controls

      Setup: you are the merchant-sailor of a small ship out of Kilwa Kisiwani, east Africa, in 1325 CE. Your crew, hull, stores, hold, and reputation are already set. You make one complete crossing there and back.

      • ADSteer port or starboard
      • WSReef or fill the lateen sails
      • SpaceBrace through rough water
      • MOpen the route chart
      • PPause · J journal
      • MouseClick the sea to steer there

      ⚓Turn flow & resources

      Each turn of play: sail → read the sea → meet a decision → pay its cost → see its consequence later.

      • HullDamage slows the ship and makes every later hit worse. Repairs cost stores.
      • SuppliesFood and water. Spent on repairs, bribes, and delays. Empty stores end the voyage.
      • CoinsBuys repairs, protection, and return cargo at Calicut.
      • CargoSix hold spaces. Weight slows you; theft and damage destroy value.
      • TrustBuilt by fair dealing at ports. High trust unlocks better prices and safer routes.
      • WindSail with the arrow to gain speed; against it you lose ground. Seasons reverse.

      ✦Decisions & reasoning

      Every decision gives three responses and never tells you which is best. Each changes the route, the stores, the hull, or the relationships you carry home. A good response can still fail if the ship is already damaged; that is cause and effect, not randomness.

      • Cause → effectYour earlier choices are recorded and reapplied later.
      • ComparisonCompare ocean, Silk Road, and desert networks on the chart.
      • ContextualizationJudge each choice against season, hull, stores, and politics.
      • ContinuityThe ending compares who left Kilwa with who came back.

      ✦Historical context

      Around 1325, merchants of the Swahili coast and the Malabar shore timed their crossings to the reversing monsoon. Crews read the sky, the stars, and a magnetic needle, and traded in ports where several languages, faiths, and currencies met.

      The same connected routes that carried pepper and cloth also carried faiths, technologies, and — as you will see at Calicut — illness.

      ✧Winning & endings

      There is no score for money alone. Three endings are possible, and the ending report explains which chain of causes produced yours.

      • Successful expeditionReached Calicut, completed the return, sound hull, real profit, sound decisions.
      • Partial successCompleted the voyage but with heavy losses, damage, or risky choices.
      • Failed expeditionWrecked, ran out of stores, or never reached Calicut.
